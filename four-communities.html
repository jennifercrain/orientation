Community Support

You’ll be assigned at least one WGU Connect course community to moderate. Community support is a recurring responsibility, and consistent, high-quality engagement is required each week.

Complete at least two engagements per assigned course community each week. You can write one main post and one reply, or two replies.

Note. You may support a course community even if you don’t offer appointments or lead live events for that course.

Calendaring

Keep a recurring 30-minute Support Activity - Communities block on your calendar each week.

In an especially active community, you may use additional Support Activity time. After completing the activity, right-click an unused Support Activity block in Outlook and select This event to edit just that occurrence. Rename it Support Activity - Communities.

Best Practices for WGU Connect

Build a Predictable Rhythm

Set a regular time to check in, respond, and plan posts. A consistent rhythm helps students know when to look for new discussions and gives them more chances to respond to one another.

Depending on how many courses you support, you may spend up to 1.5 hours per week across your communities. When you have a topic that invites discussion, a main post and a reply are a useful weekly mix.

Vary What You Post

  • Open-ended questions or polls
  • Motivational quotes or memes
  • Progress celebrations, such as “What did you tackle this week?”
  • Study tips, time management strategies, and connections to careers or everyday life
  • Light topics such as seasonal activities, sports, or hobbies

Try a short series, such as a Motivation Monday prompt for four weeks, or a deeper discussion every two weeks or once a month. Watch which posts draw student responses and vary what you share based on what students engage with.

Use a Clear Call to Action

End each main post with a call to action that gives students a clear way to join the discussion. Ask them to share one study tip, vote in a poll, or reply with a small win. For example, “What helped you get through Unit 2? Reply with one tip a peer could try this week.”

Use open-ended questions that prompt students to reflect on a specific topic, such as study habits, time management, a recent win, or a setback.

“What’s helped you stay motivated in [module, unit, or chapter]?”

Share Your Experience

You’re a student too. Share relevant experiences and struggles honestly so students have a point of connection. Think about what you’re experiencing as a student right now, then tailor your posts to shared challenges, upcoming deadlines, or common course pain points.

“What’s been the toughest part of the course so far? For me, [module, unit, or chapter] really stretched me! I met with a Course Instructor and got clarity on [topic].”

Celebrate Wins & Progress

Recognize achievements and encourage students to share their own progress. Include small wins, such as starting a new section or organizing notes, so students at different points in the course can join in.

“Shout-out to those who submitted Task 2 this week! What’s one step you took toward your next goal?”

Make Space for Setbacks

Normalize struggle and model honesty about what feels difficult. Use a warm, encouraging tone and respond with empathy so students feel comfortable sharing without fear of judgment.

“What’s been the toughest part of this course so far?”

Acknowledge and validate what students share, then follow up with a practical strategy or a relevant WGU course resource. Use only WGU course-approved resources.

Support Students Between Posts

Reply to student questions and comments, upvote useful posts, and promote Peer Navigator services when relevant. During appointments, invite students to share questions or insights with the community.

Send a one-to-one WGU Connect message for a quick check-in, proactive support, or a relevant tip. Open Inbox at the top of the page to review your conversations.

Do Not Use AI - Be Authentic

Do not use AI to generate an entire community post. Write each post in your own words so it sounds human and reflects your voice as a Peer Navigator.

Draw on your experience as a student. Share a specific course challenge, a tip you’ve tried, or a question you’d genuinely ask a peer.

Practice with Examples

Write a main post students can easily reply to

“What helped you get through [module]? I had to break the practice questions into smaller sets before they clicked. Share one thing that worked for you so someone else can try it this week.”

Try it. Replace the module and study tip with something you’ve actually experienced. Keep the ask specific enough that a student can answer in one sentence.

Reply to a student who feels stuck

“That section can feel overwhelming when you’re trying to tackle it all at once. Which part is giving you the most trouble? If you’ve worked through this section, what helped you get started?”

Try it. Acknowledge the student’s experience, then ask a focused question that gives the student and their peers a way into the conversation.

Turn a progress celebration into a discussion

“Shout-out to everyone who submitted Task 2, started a new section, or finally organized their notes this week. What’s one small win you had, and what helped you get there?”

Try it. Name a few kinds of progress so students at different points in the course can join in.

🔑 Your 5-Step Guide to Mastering Systems Thinking

Hey everyone,

If you're feeling overwhelmed by all the reading, I put together a quick list of the 5 essential concepts and tools you need to master. They are all listed under the "Learn the Basics" section on the Systems Thinking Tips page, and they're the building blocks for the tasks!

Systems and Systems Thinking: Understand the core idea of looking at the whole picture and the relationships between parts, not just individual events.

Iceberg Model: This is for Task 1. Learn to look past a surface-level event to see the patterns and, most importantly, the underlying structure (mental models/system design) that causes them.

Behavior Over Time Graphs (BOTs): Also for Task 1. You need to select and apply the graph that accurately shows the trend of key variables in your case study over time.

Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs): Crucial for Task 2. These diagrams show the cause-and-effect relationships within a system, specifically how reinforcing (R) and balancing (B) loops create outcomes.

Systems Archetypes: The other half of Task 2. Archetypes are common, recurring CLD patterns (like "Fixes that Fail") that help you quickly identify and explain a system's common problems and leverage points.

If you know these five concepts, you've got the foundation to ace the tasks!

Crafting Effective Calls to Action

A call to action (CTA) invites students to do something specific, such as sharing a study tip or replying with a win. A clear prompt makes it easier to join the conversation.

Elements of a Strong CTA

  • Be specific. Tell students exactly what to do.
  • Offer value. Give students a reason to participate.
  • Make it easy. Ask for a response students can give quickly.
  • Give a timely reason to respond. Tie the prompt to what students are working on now.
